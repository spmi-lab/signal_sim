Fractional Fourier — Theory

Definition — Angular Integral

The Fractional Fourier Transform (FrFT) is defined as the fractional power of the classical Fourier transform. The FrFT of order $\alpha$ is expressed by means of the following kernel, with angle $\phi = \alpha\pi/2$:

$$X_\alpha(u) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K_\alpha(t,u)\,x(t)\,dt$$
$$K_\alpha(t,u) = \sqrt{\tfrac{1-j\cot\phi}{2\pi}}\, \exp\!\Bigl[j\bigl(\tfrac{t^2+u^2}{2}\cot\phi - tu\csc\phi\bigr)\Bigr]$$

Special cases: $\alpha = 0$ yields the identity operator (the signal itself), $\alpha = 1$ the classical Fourier transform $X(\Omega)$, $\alpha = 2$ the time-reversal $x(-t)$, and $\alpha = 3$ the inverse Fourier transform. The operator is 4-periodic, i.e. $F^4 = I$.

Optical Interpretation — Lens Plane

Lohmann (1993) demonstrated that Fresnel diffraction through a thin lens is equivalent to the FrFT. The observation plane located at a distance $z \in [0, f]$ behind a lens of focal length $f$ produces a two-dimensional 2D FrFT of order $\alpha = z/f$.

  • $\alpha = 0$ (at the lens plane): the image itself is obtained.
  • $\alpha < 1$ (at intermediate distances): blurred intermediate FrFT images appear.
  • $\alpha = 1$ (at the focal plane): the Fourier magnitude spectrum $|F\{f\}|$ is obtained.

On this page a 2D separable FrFT is used: the one-dimensional Ozaktas–Kutay chirp–FFT–chirp algorithm is applied first to the rows and then to the columns.

Rotation in the Time–Frequency Plane

The FrFT rotates the Wigner–Ville distribution by an angle $\phi = \alpha\pi/2$:

$$W_{X_\alpha}(u, v) = W_x\!\bigl(u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi,\ u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi\bigr).$$

Accordingly, the time axis is recovered for $\alpha = 0$ and the frequency axis for $\alpha = 1$. Intermediate values yield a mixed representation along a line in the $t$–$\Omega$ plane. The compass on the right side displays the corresponding angle in real time.

Eigenfunctions: the eigenfunctions of the FrFT are the Hermite–Gauss functions. A linear chirp is compressed into an impulse at a suitable value of $\alpha$, which constitutes the fundamental application of the FrFT in radar systems performing chirp compression.

Ozaktas–Kutay Fast Algorithm

The discrete FrFT used on this page is computed as a chirp–FFT–chirp composition over the interval $|\alpha| \in [0.5, 1.5]$:

$$X_\alpha[u] = A_\phi\,e^{j\pi u^2\cot\phi} \cdot \mathcal{F}\!\bigl\{e^{j\pi t^2\cot\phi}\,x(t)\bigr\} \cdot e^{j\pi u^2\cot\phi}\Big|_{\text{sampled}}.$$

For $|\alpha| < 0.5$, the identity $F^\alpha = F^{\alpha-1}F$ is invoked recursively; at the limiting values $\alpha \to 0$ and $\alpha \to 2$, the identity and the time-reversal operation are obtained analytically. The overall computational complexity remains at $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$.

Application Areas

  • Radar and sonar: LFM chirp detection, target–clutter separation and moving-target detection (MTD) are carried out using the FrFT.
  • Optics: semi-Fourier transforms obtained via thin lenses, phase-free holograms and diffraction analyses are conducted within this framework.
  • Filtering: filtering along oblique bands in the Wigner plane is effectively performed by the FrFT.
  • Image encryption: since the parameter $\alpha$ acts as a key, the FrFT is employed in image-encryption schemes.
  • Signal compression: the FrFT provides a much more compact representation than the DCT for signals belonging to the chirp family.

Usage Tips

  • In the 2D panel, a "semi-Fourier" appearance is observed for $\alpha \approx 0.5$.
  • In the Chirp tab, keeping $k$ fixed and varying $\alpha$ produces, at a particular value of $\alpha$, an imaginary component that vanishes while the real component is concentrated into an impulse.
  • When an audio file is uploaded, the signal length should be kept short (approximately 0.5 s); otherwise the computational cost of the FrFT increases substantially.
  • The Cartesian compass on the right side visualises how the $(t, \Omega)$ axis rotates as $\alpha$ is varied.
