Theoretical Background

A summary of signal classification, the underlying mathematical definitions and the principles governing the behaviours observed in the simulation is provided below.

Periodic & Aperiodic Signals

A signal $x(t)$ is periodic if, for the smallest $T_0 > 0$, at every $t$

$$x(t + T_0) = x(t), \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R}$$

holds; $T_0$ is the fundamental period, $F_0 = 1/T_0$ is the fundamental frequency, and $\Omega_0 = 2\pi F_0$ is the angular fundamental frequency. Otherwise the signal is aperiodic.

  • example $\sin(\Omega_0 t)$ → periodic, $T_0 = 2\pi/\Omega_0$
  • example $e^{-\alpha t}u(t)$ → aperiodic
  • The sum of two periodic signals is periodic only if $T_1/T_2$ is rational.

Dirac Delta $\delta(t)$

The Dirac delta is a generalized function (distribution); it is not a function in the classical sense. It is defined by two fundamental properties:

$$\delta(t) = 0,\; t \neq 0; \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t)\,dt = 1$$

Sifting (sampling) property: for any continuous $f(t)$

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,\delta(t - t_0)\,dt = f(t_0).$$
  • Scaling: $\delta(at) = \dfrac{1}{|a|}\delta(t)$
  • Evenness: $\delta(-t) = \delta(t)$
  • Derivative: $u'(t) = \delta(t)$ (in the distributional sense)
  • The impulse response $h(t)$ of LTI systems is defined as the response to a $\delta(t)$ input.

Unit Step $u(t)$ (Heaviside)

$$u(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t \geq 0 \\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}$$

Derivative: $u'(t) = \delta(t)$ — the derivative of the step is the Dirac delta. Integral: $\int u(\tau)d\tau = r(t)$ — integrating the step yields the ramp.

The unit step is regarded as the building block of causal signals: the expression $x(t)\,u(t)$ extracts only the $t \geq 0$ portion of $x(t)$. Switching events and sources activated at $t=0$ are modelled by means of this signal.

Exponential & Sinusoidal Signals

Two fundamental eigenfunctions of continuous time:

$$x(t) = A e^{\alpha t}, \qquad x(t) = A\cos(\Omega_0 t + \varphi)$$

These two representations are unified through Euler's identity:

$$e^{j\Omega t} = \cos(\Omega t) + j\sin(\Omega t).$$

The generalised complex exponential $A\,e^{s t}$, with $s = \sigma + j\Omega$, gives rise to either decaying or growing oscillations and constitutes the mathematical foundation of the Laplace transform and of the natural response of LTI systems. For $\sigma < 0$ a damped behaviour is observed, $\sigma = 0$ yields a pure oscillation, and $\sigma > 0$ corresponds to a growing oscillation.

Energy & Power Signals

$$E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt, \qquad P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2 dt$$
  • Energy signal: $0 < E < \infty$ and $P = 0$. Typical: short pulses, $e^{-\alpha t}u(t)$.
  • Power signal: $0 < P < \infty$ and $E = \infty$. Typical: $\cos(\Omega_0 t)$, periodic signals.
  • A signal cannot be both an energy and a power signal (except for the Dirac).

The power of a periodic signal can also be defined over a single period: $P = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{T_0}|x(t)|^2 dt$.

Even & Odd Components

Any $x(t)$ can be uniquely decomposed into even and odd parts:

$$x_e(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[x(t) + x(-t)\big],\quad x_o(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[x(t) - x(-t)\big]$$

The relations $x_e(-t) = x_e(t)$ (even) and $x_o(-t) = -x_o(t)$ (odd) hold, with $x(t) = x_e(t) + x_o(t)$. This decomposition constitutes the basis of the symmetry properties of the Fourier transform: the Fourier transform of a real-valued and even signal is itself real-valued and even, whereas that of a real-valued and odd signal is purely imaginary and odd.

Time-Domain Operations

The fundamental signal operations listed below correspond directly to the shift, scaling and amplitude controls available in the simulation:

  • Time shift: $x(t - t_0)$ — $t_0 > 0$ shifts to the right (delay).
  • Time scaling: $x(at)$ — $|a| > 1$ compresses, $|a| < 1$ expands.
  • Time reversal: $x(-t)$ — reflection, at the core of convolution and correlation.
  • Amplitude scaling: $A \cdot x(t)$.
  • General transformation: $x(at - b) = x(a(t - b/a))$ — first scale, then shift.

Dirac Comb $\delta_T(t)$

$$\delta_T(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t - kT)$$

This signal, defined as an impulse train of period $T$, constitutes the mathematical core of the sampling theorem: the multiplication of a continuous signal by $\delta_T(t)$ yields a discrete-time representation composed of the values $x(kT)$.

The Fourier transform of this signal is again a Dirac comb (Poisson summation formula):

$$\mathcal{F}\{\delta_T(t)\} = \frac{2\pi}{T}\sum_{k} \delta\!\left(\Omega - \tfrac{2\pi k}{T}\right).$$

Signal Classification — Summary

  • Continuous- and discrete-time: $x(t)$ versus $x[n]$ — the continuous-time case is addressed on this page.
  • Periodic and aperiodic: the presence or absence of a repeating structure.
  • Even and odd: the symmetry relation with $x(-t)$.
  • Energy and power signals: whether the integral of $|x(t)|^2$ is bounded or not.
  • Causal / anticausal / two-sided: whether the signal takes non-zero values for $t<0$.
  • Deterministic and random: whether the signal admits a closed-form description.
  • Analogue and digital: whether the amplitude is continuous or quantised.

Each signal may belong to several classes simultaneously; for instance, $\cos(\Omega_0 t)$ is classified as continuous, periodic, even, a power signal, deterministic and two-sided.
