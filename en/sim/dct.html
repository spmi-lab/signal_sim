Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) — Theoretical Background

1. The Motivation for the DCT

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) represents a signal as a sum of sinusoidal and cosinusoidal components and presupposes a periodic extension. When a finite-length signal is treated as periodic, a jump arises between the first and last samples. This discontinuity gives rise, in the frequency domain, to pronounced high-frequency side lobes (Gibbs phenomenon); the energy of the signal is dispersed over a large number of coefficients and the resulting compression is inefficient.

The DCT eliminates this drawback by extending the signal with even symmetry. Under such an extension no discontinuity arises and the signal continues smoothly to infinity. The resulting energy-compaction property concentrates the bulk of the signal energy into a small number of low-frequency coefficients. This property has made the DCT the fundamental component of standards such as JPEG, MP3, MPEG, H.264/H.265, HEIC, AAC, and WebP. From a theoretical standpoint, the DCT provides a close approximation to the Karhunen–Loève transform (KLT) for first-order Markov signals: the KLT achieves optimal decorrelation but is signal-dependent, whereas the DCT attains nearly the same performance with a fixed basis.

2. DCT Types and Formulas

Since Ahmed, Natarajan, and Rao's original 1974 paper, eight different DCT types have been defined (DCT-I through DCT-VIII). The three most commonly used in practice:

DCT-I: Reflects the samples at the signal endpoints with whole-sample symmetry (period $2(N-1)$).

$$X[k] = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(x[0] + (-1)^k x[N{-}1]\bigr) + \sum_{n=1}^{N-2} x[n]\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi k n}{N-1}\right], \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$$

DCT-II: Half-sample symmetric extension (period $2N$). The most common type — used by MP3, JPEG, MPEG.

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi}{N}\!\left(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)k\right], \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$$

DCT-III: The inverse of DCT-II (up to a scale factor). "Inverse DCT" (IDCT) generally refers to this.

$$x[n] = \tfrac{1}{2}X[0] + \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} X[k]\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi}{N}\!\left(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)k\right], \quad n = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$$

Orthonormal form: The formulas above are in "classical" notation. In the orthonormal version, $X[k]$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{2/N}$ (and $\sqrt{1/N}$ for $k=0$); in this case the DCT matrix $\mathbf{C}$ is orthogonal, i.e., $\mathbf{C}^T\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I}$. In practice, this ensures Parseval's theorem holds and simplifies quantization analysis.

3. Fast Algorithm — Relationship with the FFT

Direct computation of the DCT-II is of $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ complexity. However, as shown by Makhoul in 1980, an $N$-point DCT-II can be computed using a $2N$-point FFT through $$X[k] = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{e^{-j\pi k/(2N)} \cdot \mathrm{FFT}_{2N}\bigl(\tilde{x}\bigr)[k]\right\},$$ with $\tilde{x}[n] = x[n]$ for $n < N$ and $\tilde{x}[n] = 0$ for $n \ge N$. This reduces the complexity to $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$. On this page, the same FFT-based approach is used for DCT-I, DCT-II, and DCT-III; even a 7-second audio segment is processed in approximately 100 ms.

4. Symmetric Extension and Periodicity

The "symmetric extension" visualised on the second screen lies at the mathematical origin of the DCT. The symmetry structure of the three principal types is as follows:

  • DCT-I: the sequence $x[0], x[1], \dots, x[N-1], x[N-2], \dots, x[1]$ is repeated; the endpoints are reflected with whole-sample symmetry.
  • DCT-II: the sequence $x[0], x[1], \dots, x[N-1], x[N-1], x[N-2], \dots, x[0]$ is repeated; the endpoints are reflected with half-sample symmetry.
  • DCT-III: exhibits an anti-symmetric half-sample structure and constitutes the frequency-domain counterpart of the DCT-II.

5. Compression — Top-K and Low-Pass Approaches

Storing all $N$ DCT coefficients leaves the data size unchanged and does not constitute compression. Compression is achieved by retaining only the most significant coefficients and setting the remainder to zero. Two different thresholding schemes are provided on this page:

  • Top-K (the largest $K$ coefficients): the coefficients are sorted according to $|X[k]|$ and the largest $K$ of them are retained. This approach minimises the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and yields the highest SNR.
  • Low-pass (the first $K$ coefficients): only the coefficients with $k = 0, 1, \dots, K-1$ are retained. High-frequency components are discarded, which performs satisfactorily for speech signals but suppresses the upper harmonics in music.

The compression ratio is defined as $N/K$. In practice, JPEG and MP3 incorporate not only thresholding but also quantisation and entropy coding (Huffman, arithmetic coding). This page focuses on the thresholding step, which constitutes the first stage of practical codecs.

6. Sampling Rate and Anti-Aliasing

According to the Nyquist theorem, the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency present in the signal in order to represent it without loss of information. A sampling rate of 8 kHz captures frequencies up to 4 kHz (sufficient for telephony); 16 kHz captures frequencies up to 8 kHz (ample for speech); and 44.1 kHz captures frequencies up to 22 kHz (CD quality, above the upper limit of human hearing).

On this page, an anti-aliasing filter is applied when a high-rate signal is converted to a lower rate: an ideal low-pass filter is first applied in the frequency domain so that components above the Nyquist limit are removed, after which sample decimation is performed. In the absence of this filtering step, high frequencies would appear as low frequencies (aliasing) and the signal would be corrupted.

📍 Application Areas

  • JPEG/HEIC: The foundation of image compression — 2D DCT on 8x8 blocks, then quantization + entropy coding. All digital photographs.
  • MP3/AAC/Vorbis: Audio compression — perceptual coding with Modified DCT (MDCT); discards frequencies humans cannot hear.
  • MPEG-1/2/4 (video): DCT for each frame; more advanced integer DCT in H.264/HEVC.
  • WebP, AVIF: Modern web formats — transforms related to DCT.
  • Image watermarking: Embedding information in mid-frequency DCT coefficients (steganography).
  • Machine learning: Classical feature extraction — DCT coefficients in face recognition (pre-eigenfaces).
  • Medical image compression: In DICOM, mammography, MRI compression standards.
  • Bandwidth savings: Image transfer from spacecraft/satellites to Earth.
